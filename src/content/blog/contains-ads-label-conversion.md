**Key points:**
- The "Contains ads" label is a pre-install note. It's separate from how often a user actually hits an ad once they're using the app.
- Its real effect on conversion likely shifts by category, mattering more where ad-free apps are common and less where ads are already expected.
- The label and the real ad experience are two different things that can drift apart, a light label with heavy ads, or a disclosed label with a genuinely light experience.
- Dropping ads just to lose the label is a real tradeoff, not an easy win, since it touches your revenue directly.

A small label sitting quietly on your listing makes a promise, or a warning, before a user ever opens your app.

## What the label actually is

The label tells a user one fact. This app shows ads. It says nothing about how often, how pushy, or how annoying those ads actually feel once someone is using the app. A user reading the label before installing works off that one bit of information, not any real sense of what the ad experience will feel like. The label and the real experience are two separate things, and they don't always match.

| | What it tells a user |
|---|---|
| "Contains ads" label | This app shows ads, nothing more specific |
| The real in-app experience | How often, how annoying, which kind of ads a user actually hits |

## Why its real effect likely depends on category

A user browsing a category where ad apps are the clear norm has probably already set their own expectations before they even see the label. It barely counts as news. A user browsing a category where paid, ad-free apps are common might read the same label a different way. A real reason to pick the ad-free option sitting right next to it. The same label can carry very different weight, all based on what a user already expects walking in.

> **Real-world scenario:** A team worried their "Contains ads" label was quietly costing them installs and checked it against their own conversion numbers from before ads were added. The rate barely moved after the label appeared, since their category was already full of ad-supported apps users expected going in. A different team, in a category where most rivals were paid and ad-free, saw a real dip after adding ads of their own, since the label stood out against an otherwise ad-free field.

## Why this isn't a reason to drop ads on its own

Dropping ads to lose the label touches your real revenue, a genuine tradeoff with its own costs well past conversion. The label's effect is one thing to weigh, not a final answer. A category where the label barely matters doesn't mean you should keep ads you'd otherwise want to drop for other reasons, and a category where it matters more doesn't automatically mean dropping ads is the right call either.

## What to actually do about it

1. **Check what's normal in your own category before assuming the label is costing you installs.** Its real effect likely hinges on what users there already expect.
2. **Check your own numbers from before and after ads, if you have them.** That beats guessing from the label alone.
3. **Treat dropping ads as a revenue call, not a conversion fix.** The label is one input, not the whole picture, in a decision that touches your income directly.
4. **Keep the label's effect separate from the real ad experience.** They're different things, and fixing one doesn't fix the other on its own.
