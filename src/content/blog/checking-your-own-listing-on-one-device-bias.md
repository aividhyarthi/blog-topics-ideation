**Key points:**
- Your own phone is one specific device, not a stand-in for every screen size and OS version your real users carry.
- A layout issue or cut-off element that only shows up on a different device can go unseen forever if nobody checks past their own.
- Checking doesn't mean owning every device. A few borrowed devices, an emulator, or a colleague on different hardware close real gaps.
- The real risk isn't that your check is wrong. It's that it's incomplete in a way that's easy to forget.

Pulling up your own listing on your own phone feels like a solid, direct check. It's solid for exactly one device, and that may not be the one that matters.

## Why one device isn't a real sample

Checking your listing on your own phone tells you one thing clearly. How it looks on that specific screen size, that specific OS version, maybe even shaped by your own account's saved settings. Your real user base spans a real range of devices and OS versions you don't personally own. A check on your own device answers a narrower question than the one that actually matters. How does this look across the devices real users are actually on.

| | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Checking your own device | How your listing looks on exactly one device |
| Checking a range of devices | Whether an issue is everywhere or just on one setup |

## Why this gap is easy to miss

A listing checked on your own phone looks fine, because it probably is fine on that one device. That success feels like a full check, since nothing visibly broke. The gap stays hidden exactly because your own device doesn't happen to trigger whatever issue a different screen size or OS version would show. You're not making a mistake by checking your own device. You're just not checking the thing that would actually surface a device-specific problem.

> **Real-world scenario:** A team checked their listing on the team's own phones before every update and never spotted a problem, since everything looked clean each time. A user reported a key screenshot looked cropped in a confusing way on their specific, older device. Checking an emulator set to match that device's screen, the team saw the exact same cropping right away, something none of their own phones, all newer models, had ever shown. The listing had been fine on every device they'd personally checked. It just wasn't fine on every device their real users actually had.

## What checking more broadly actually takes

This doesn't mean owning every device on the market. A small, deliberate set of checks, a borrowed older device, a free emulator set to a different screen size, a colleague with different hardware taking one look, closes a real amount of the gap a single personal device leaves open. The goal isn't full coverage. It's not relying on one sample to stand in for your entire real user base.

## What to actually do about it

1. **Don't treat a check on your own device as a full check.** It's one real data point, not a stand-in for your whole user base.
2. **Use at least one extra device or emulator setup regularly.** It doesn't need to be exhaustive to catch a real device-specific issue your own phone would never show.
3. **Ask a colleague on different hardware to take an occasional look.** A second device you don't personally own is often the easiest gap to close.
4. **Take a user report of a device-specific issue seriously, even if you can't see it on your own phone.** Your device looking fine doesn't mean theirs does.
