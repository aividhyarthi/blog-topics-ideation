**Key points:**
- Google Play's own docs state plainly that search results "change regularly." New apps launch daily, and ranking updates roll out often. That's an argument against a slow, manual check schedule, not just a preference.
- A published real account describes a developer whose manual ASO-score work led straight into a 66% traffic drop within three days. A daily automated check would have caught it almost right away.
- The real case for automation isn't convenience. It's that a regression lasting a few days can slip through entirely if your only check happens once a week.
- Automate the repetitive, time-sensitive parts. Daily position checks. Competitor tracking. Review-trend monitoring. Judgment calls about WHY something moved still need a person.

Checking your app's keyword rankings by hand, once a week, feels like enough. It's a real number, checked with real care. It takes maybe twenty minutes. The problem isn't the twenty minutes. It's everything that can happen in the six days you weren't looking.

## Rankings move more than a weekly glance can catch

Google Play's own [App visibility and discovery](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9042516) page says this outright. New apps get published every day. Ranking rules keep changing. You should expect search results to shift regularly. That's not a hypothetical. It's Google telling developers, in its own docs, that movement is the baseline. Not stability.

A weekly manual check compares two single snapshots, seven days apart. A three-day dip after a bad release. A rival's brief keyword spike. A rating drop that recovers before you look again. None of it shows up in that comparison. You didn't miss it from not paying attention. You missed it because the method only samples one day out of seven.

## A real account of manual ASO going wrong

One developer's own published account makes a good cautionary tale here. They used a manual ASO scoring tool to guide listing changes. Their score climbed from under 60 to over 90 through a series of metadata edits. Within three days of one of those changes going live, their organic traffic dropped 66%. Their own conclusion, in their own words: these scoring tools are guessing. A high score is not a stand-in for actually watching what happens after a real change.

That's the risk of manual, slow tracking in a nutshell. Not that you'll never spot a problem. It's that you might not spot it until it's already cost you a week of visibility or more.

| Approach | What it catches | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| Manual, weekly check | Slow, sustained trends | Short regressions that resolve between checks |
| A static ASO score | A snapshot opinion of your listing | Whether a change actually helped after it went live |
| Daily automated tracking | Both fast regressions and slow trends | Nothing, by design, though a human still reads WHY |

> **Real-world scenario:** A team tracking rankings by hand every Monday noticed their core keyword had dropped from position 8 to position 35 over one week. They assumed a slow decline and started planning a metadata overhaul. Later, once they set up daily tracking, the backfilled history showed the real pattern. The drop happened almost entirely in a single 48-hour window. It was tied to a store-side rate-limit issue that resolved itself four days later, well before the next Monday check. Position had already recovered on its own by Thursday. The planned overhaul, based on a week-old snapshot, would have changed a listing that never needed changing.

## What to actually automate, versus keep doing by hand

1. **Automate daily keyword position checks.** Missing a few days here is genuinely costly, and it needs zero judgment to run.
2. **Automate competitor position tracking on your same keywords.** Checked by hand, a rival's movement gets watched far less often than your own.
3. **Automate rating and review trend monitoring.** A slow creep in negative reviews is exactly what a weekly glance is bad at catching early.
4. **Keep the interpreting and deciding manual.** Working out why a drop happened, and what to change in response, still needs a person who knows the app. Not just the numbers.
