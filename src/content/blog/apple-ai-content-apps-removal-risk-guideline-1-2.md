**Key points:**
- Apple's own [App Review Guidelines, rule 1.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) cover apps where users "create, generate, upload, share, or otherwise contribute content." The rule names "AI content creation apps" by name, right next to social apps.
- The same rule names two specific harms it targets: nonconsensual intimate imagery, and bullying.
- Four things are required, per Apple's own text: filter bad content before it posts, let people report it, let people block abusive users, and publish real contact info.
- A separate, harsher rule, in Apple's own Section 5, covers trafficking and child exploitation. The stated result there is removal and a report to the authorities.

A new app category just got named, by name, in Apple's own removal rules. Right next to social apps and random chat apps.

## What Apple's own rule actually says

Apple's own text covers apps where users "create, generate, upload, share, or otherwise contribute content." It says these apps face real risks. Apple names three. Stolen work. Nonconsensual intimate imagery. Bullying. Then it names three app types, by name. Social apps. UGC apps. AI content creation apps.

| Detail | What Apple's own rule says |
|---|---|
| Named app types | Social apps, UGC apps, AI content creation apps |
| Named risks | Stolen work, nonconsensual intimate imagery, bullying |
| Must build: filtering | A way to stop bad content before it posts |
| Must build: reporting | A way for users to report it, with a real reply |
| Must build: blocking | A way to block abusive users |
| Must build: contact | Real, published contact info |

## Why naming AI apps by name actually matters

Old UGC rules did the heavy lifting here. An app that made images, instead of just hosting ones users uploaded, could argue it sat in a gray zone. Apple's own new wording closes that gap. It names AI content apps in the same breath as social apps and UGC apps. Same four things apply to all three. If your app makes content from a prompt, not just hosts what a user uploads, this rule hits you directly. Not by a stretch.

> **Real-world scenario:** A small team built a photo app with one AI feature. It turned a selfie into a stylized portrait. They'd always treated their safety checklist as something for social apps and chat apps. Not an editing tool. Reading Apple's own new text, they saw their own feature named outright. AI content creation apps. Not a stretch at all. They added a report flow and a block feature before their next release. They didn't want to learn the hard way, through a rejection, that filtering alone wasn't enough.

## The separate, harsher rule worth knowing

Apple's own Section 5 says this plainly. An app "found to facilitate human trafficking and/or the exploitation of children" gets three things. Police get told. The app gets pulled from the App Store. It gets blocked from other stores too. A second rule, 1.1.4, adds one more line. Porn "may include... human trafficking and exploitation" as a named example of bad content. This sits apart from the UGC rules above. No checklist fixes a finding like this. The stated result is removal. Not a request to add a feature.

## What to actually do about it

1. **Check if your app's features match "create, generate, upload, share, or contribute" at all.** That's a wide net. Not just apps that call themselves social.
2. **Build all four things, not just one or two.** Filtering alone, without a report flow and a block feature, likely isn't enough.
3. **Treat AI-made content the same as content a user uploads.** Apple's own words draw no real line between the two.
4. **Don't treat the trafficking rule as a checklist item.** It's a separate, much harsher standard. There's no fix once it's found.
