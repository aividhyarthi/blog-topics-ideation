**Key points:**
- A price range shows the full spread of possible in-app purchases, from the cheapest to the most expensive.
- A wide range can draw attention to its high end, even if most real purchases happen near the low end.
- Whether this helps or hurts a first impression likely depends on the app and its real purchase pattern.
- Checking where your real purchases cluster is the useful next step, not assuming the range itself is good or bad.

A single line showing a price range sits on almost every listing with in-app purchases, and it rarely gets a second look once it's set.

## What the range is actually saying

A price range tells a new user the full spread of what they might spend, from the smallest purchase up to the biggest one offered anywhere in the app. That's useful, honest information. It also means one high-priced option, even one most users never touch, can push the visible top of that range far past what a typical purchase actually costs. The range is accurate. Whether it matches what most users actually spend is a different question entirely.

| | What it shows | What it might imply |
|---|---|---|
| A narrow range | A tight, predictable cost | Reads as clear and low-commitment |
| A wide range | The full spread, top to bottom | The top number can read as the "real" cost, even if rarely bought |

## Why a wide range can work against a first impression

A user glancing at a listing for the first time doesn't yet know how your purchases actually spread out. They see the range as shown, and the higher number can shape their first guess at what the app might cost them, even if almost every real purchase lands much closer to the low end. This effect isn't guaranteed, but it's a real possibility worth checking against your own real data instead of assuming the range is harmless just because it's accurate.

> **Real-world scenario:** A team noticed their listing showed a wide price range because of one high-end bundle built mainly for a small group of power users. Checking their real purchase data, almost every real sale landed near the bottom of that range, nowhere close to the number setting their visible ceiling. They couldn't be sure the wide range was costing them sales without a direct test, but the gap between what the range implied and what users actually spent was worth knowing either way.

## What's actually worth checking

The useful question isn't whether price ranges are good or bad in general. It's where your own real purchases actually land. A range that closely matches typical spending is simply accurate and probably costing you nothing. A range stretched wide by a rarely-bought top item might be overstating the real cost to a first-time viewer, which is worth knowing even if you decide not to change your pricing at all.

## What to actually do about it

1. **Check where your real purchases cluster, not just what your full range shows.** That tells you whether the shown range actually matches typical spending.
2. **Don't assume a wide range is automatically hurting you.** It might be accurate and harmless, depending on your real purchase data.
3. **Look at what's driving your range's high end.** A single rare, high-ticket item can set a ceiling that doesn't reflect what most users will actually pay.
4. **Treat this as a data question before a pricing change.** Understand your real numbers first, change the pricing structure second, if it turns out to be worth it at all.
