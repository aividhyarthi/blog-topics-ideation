**Key points:**
- Tablet users expect to rotate the screen more freely than phone users, who are used to a fixed portrait default.
- A listing rarely shows orientation support clearly, so most users find out about a locked orientation only after they install.
- That makes this more of a retention issue than a conversion issue for most apps.
- Whether it's worth fixing depends on how many of your real users are actually on tablets.

A locked portrait screen barely registers on a phone. On a tablet, the same lock can feel like a real limit.

## Why phones and tablets feel different here

A phone's default is portrait, and most users never think about it, since nearly every app works the same way. A tablet is a different object. Often laid flat, propped up, or turned depending on what the user is doing right then. An app locked to one orientation can feel more restrictive on a tablet than the same lock would on a phone. The tablet user's own habits already involve more turning the screen to begin with.

| | What's typical |
|---|---|
| Phone | Portrait by default, rarely questioned |
| Tablet | Orientation shifts with the task, a lock feels more restrictive |

## Why this rarely shows up before install

A listing's screenshots don't usually make orientation support clear one way or the other. A user browsing the store has little way to know ahead of time whether an app will turn with them or lock them in place. So when the mismatch exists, it tends to show up only after install. A real letdown, just a delayed one, not something that shaped the download in the first place.

> **Real-world scenario:** A team built their app locked to portrait, a reasonable call for a mostly phone-based user base, and didn't think much about how it would feel on a tablet. Checking reviews from tablet users, a complaint kept coming up. Not being able to turn the screen while using the app propped up on a stand. Nothing on the listing had misled anyone. The gap only showed up once tablet users were already inside the app, past the point where a listing could have warned them.

## What this means for deciding whether to fix it

The real question is how many of your real users are on tablets at all. A small tablet share makes full orientation support harder to justify against other work. A real tablet share changes that math, since the mismatch is shaping how a sizable chunk of your users actually experience the app, even if it never shows up as a number you can track before install.

## What to actually do about it

1. **Check how many of your real users are on tablets before you prioritize this.** That number decides how much the mismatch actually matters.
2. **Look at reviews and support messages for complaints about turning the screen.** That's usually where this shows up, since it rarely appears in pre-install data.
3. **Don't expect your listing to flag this limit for you.** Most users won't know until after they install, which makes it a retention issue more than a conversion one.
4. **Weigh the fix against your real tablet numbers, not a guess either way.** This isn't a must-fix for everyone. It depends on your actual users.
